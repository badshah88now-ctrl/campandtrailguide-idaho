Camping in Idaho: Sawtooths, Hot Springs and Wide-Open Country

Idaho is one of the most underrated camping states in the American West. The jagged Sawtooth Mountains, deep river canyons, natural hot springs, and huge tracts of national forest give you room to breathe — and far fewer crowds than the neighbouring states. A well-planned Idaho trip mixes mountain lakes, riverside camps, and a soak in a hot spring at the end of the day.

The Sawtooth Mountains

The Sawtooths are Idaho's crown jewel: sharp granite peaks rising over alpine lakes like Redfish Lake and Stanley Lake. Summer is the main season here, with wildflowers in July and August and larch trees turning gold in late September. Campgrounds around Stanley fill on summer weekends, so reserve ahead or arrive midweek. Mornings are cold even in August — frost is possible at higher elevations — so pack a warm sleeping bag and a good pad. Afternoon thunderstorms roll through regularly, so set up camp early and keep rain gear within reach.

Rivers and canyons

Idaho's rivers are legendary. The Salmon River, the Middle Fork, and the Snake River through Hells Canyon — the deepest river gorge in North America — offer rafting, fishing, and riverside camping that is hard to match anywhere. Riverside campgrounds can be hot in summer, so bring shade and plenty of water. The canyon country around Hells Canyon is best in spring and fall, when temperatures are mild and the rattlesnakes are less active. Always check river levels and permit rules before a multi-day float trip.

Hot springs and high desert

Idaho has more soakable hot springs than almost any other state. Many are a short walk from a forest road, and a few sit right beside campgrounds — a warm soak after a long hike is one of camping's great pleasures. Treat hot springs with respect: test the water before getting in, never soak alone in remote pools, and leave no trace. South of the mountains, the high desert around Craters of the Moon offers a strange, volcanic landscape of lava flows and cinder cones — camp there in spring or fall, when the black rock is not baking in summer heat.

Planning your Idaho trip

Whatever part of Idaho you choose, a little planning goes a long way. The CampAndTrailGuide trail map helps you find campgrounds and trailheads across the state, and the beginner's camping checklist makes sure nothing essential stays at home. Start your planning at the CampAndTrailGuide homepage, check fire restrictions and road conditions before you go, and give yourself an extra day — Idaho's distances are bigger than they look on the map.

Idaho rewards campers who take the time to explore it. Pack for cold mountain mornings, hot canyon afternoons, and everything in between, and you will find a state that still feels wild.
